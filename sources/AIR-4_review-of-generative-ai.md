## Content (full text — abstract confirmed against PDF)

- **Problem / domain:** Healthcare data scarcity, privacy and heterogeneity block ML development, so generative models are used to synthesise imaging, EHR/tabular, genomic, text and signal data. Review of GANs, VAEs, Transformers and diffusion models plus their federated/privacy-preserving variants.
- **Big-data context:** Secondary evidence only — "No datasets were generated or analysed during the current study" (p. 60). Scale claims come from cited studies: 250,000+ patient EHRs (EVA, p. 19), 15M PubMed abstracts (BioGPT, p. 25), 20 hospitals in a federated deployment (DSL, p. 51), 2048×2048 synthetic images (ProGAN, p. 13). No big-data platform is used or recommended; Google Colab and Vertex AI appear once as environments cited from an included study (p. 15).
- **ML methods / architecture:** Five families compared, each with a variant catalogue: GANs (DCGAN, cGAN, WGAN, CycleGAN, ACGAN, ProGAN, AttnGAN, SAGAN, BigGAN, FSGAN, StyleGAN1/2/2-ADA/T), VAEs (CVAE, sparse/stacked/recurrent/contractive, VQ-VAE, β-VAE, VGAE), Transformers (GPT-3/4, BioGPT, ViT/ResViT, BART, MaskGIT, MAGVIT, DALL-E 1–3, Perceiver IO), diffusion (DDPM, LDM, PDDM, ADM, TBDM, ARDM, D3PM, TransFusion, DiffInfinite), federated generative (FedCGAN, FLIGAN, FedVAE, FedMed-GAN, AsynDGAN, Fed3DGS, PPPFL). Privacy machinery: DP-SGD, PATE-GAN, secure aggregation, homomorphic encryption, membership-inference resistance.
- **Review method:** Structured narrative survey, not a formal systematic review. Window 1 Jan 2018 – 20 Apr 2025; five databases (PubMed, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, arXiv, Google Scholar); two reviewers + third-reviewer arbitration; inclusion requires ≥1 utility/quality metric (FID, IS, AUROC, AUPRC, MAE, MMD) or a privacy evaluation (ε-DP, membership inference). The paper states it did not compute exclusion counts (p. 4) nor numerical quality scores (p. 6 — qualitative rubric: venue, clarity, evaluation depth, reproducibility, citations). The included-study count is never stated. Taxonomy in Figure 1 (p. 7): training paradigm (adversarial, variational, diffusion) × architecture (Transformers) × privacy extension (federated).
- **Paper structure:** §1 Introduction pp. 2–4 · §2 Taxonomy + method pp. 4–7 · §3 model families pp. 7–47 (GANs pp. 7–17, VAEs pp. 17–24, Transformers pp. 25–31, diffusion pp. 31–39, federated pp. 40–47; each family closes with a "Summary and open issues" table — Tables 3, 5, 7, 9, 11) · §4 Discussion pp. 47–60 (comparative analysis §4.1 pp. 47–53 incl. Table 12 p. 48 and Table 13 pp. 50–51; deployment §4.2 pp. 53–55; challenges/limitations §4.3 pp. 55–57; future directions §4.4 pp. 57–60) · §5 Conclusion p. 60 · References pp. 60–71.
- **Key findings:**
  - No family wins everywhere: diffusion leads image fidelity (sCT MAE 43 HU, SSIM 0.965; segmentation FID 3.6 CT / 6.5 MR); GAN augmentation gives +2.8 to +6.7 pp histopathology AUROC; VAEs win structured EHR (EHR-Safe < 3% downstream gap, KS drift < 0.03, MIA ≈ chance; PATE-GAN ε = 2 with < 0.03 AUROC drop); federated generative models trade communication cost for privacy (pp. 36, 50–53).
  - Efficiency numbers matter as much as fidelity: 20× fewer diffusion steps, ProDiff 24× faster in two steps, MaskGIT up to 48× faster, FIMI up to 50% energy reduction, FLIGAN +20% accuracy (pp. 13, 27, 33, 34, 41–42).
  - Fidelity metrics do not track clinical usefulness — "FID and SSIM … do not always align with downstream diagnostic performance or clinical trustworthiness" (p. 16).
  - Privacy is an attack-surface problem: gradient leakage, output leakage and auxiliary-data inference, each with named defences and explicit privacy–utility–cost trade-offs (pp. 55–57).
  - Regulation treats synthetic data as supplementary evidence only — HIPAA, GDPR, EU AI Act (high-risk), FDA GMLP, MHRA/NICE, WHO, OECD (pp. 56–57); synthetic data is "supplementary," not sufficient for approval (pp. 55–60).
- **Author-stated limitations:** structured survey rather than systematic review (no exclusion counts, p. 4; not tracked per stage, p. 6); no numerical quality scores (p. 6); heterogeneous benchmarks forced a mainly qualitative synthesis (pp. 6, 52); no datasets generated or analysed (p. 60).
- **Limitations we see:** the corpus size is never reported, so the "unified comparative evaluation" is not reproducible or weightable; no experiments, re-analysis or audit of its own — every number is second-hand; Table 13 mixes peer-reviewed results, preprints and abstract-only claims (e.g. DSL's "abstract reports up to ∼55%") with no seeds, confidence intervals or harmonised metrics (pp. 50–51); privacy is promised as a dimension but reported thinly — k-anonymity and distance-to-closest-record are named (p. 52) yet never appear in Table 13, and no DP budgets or leakage measurements are given; deployment discussion has no cost figures, hardware specifics, site-level or low-resource/LMIC evidence and no interoperability standards (FHIR/OMOP absent); bias is asserted rather than measured (skin tone mentioned at p. 56); coverage skews to imaging/EHR — genomics and signals are thin (one ECG and one EEG benchmark row), medical video/audio is speculative; no agentic-LLM workflows and only one forensic citation on synthetic-data detection (p. 35).
- **Datasets / benchmarks discussed:** MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, eICU (EHR synthesis, EHR-Safe < 3% gap; D3PM phenotyping +0.02–0.05 AUROC; clinical letters, BERTScore > 0.90); ISIC 2018/2019/2020 (StyleGAN2-ADA dermoscopy FID ≈ 7.02, KID 0.24%, AUC ~98.8% vs ~98.6–98.8% augmented); BraTS/BraTS2021 + IXI (FedMed-GAN PSNR 28.3 federated vs 27.1 centralized, SSIM 0.989 vs 0.986); Medical Segmentation Decathlon (FLWGAN); IU-Xray (report generation, CLIP 0.78, BLEU-4 0.34); Isfahan MISP + hospital HRA fundus (fundus→FFA PSNR 25.16 dB / SSIM 0.8268 vs CycleGAN 19.65 / 0.5799); seg2med benchmark (SSIM 0.94 CT / 0.89 MR, FID 3.6 / 6.5); UCR Time Series Archive (sparse-VAE augmentation); beat-level ECG (TSTR AUROC 0.89 synthetic vs 0.91 real); single-channel EEG sleep staging (F1 ≈ 0.87 vs 0.89 real); tabular health registry (PATE-GAN ε = 2, AUROC drop < 0.03). Non-medical proxies: CelebA/CelebA-HQ, CIFAR-10, MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, VGG-Faces, miniImageNet.
- **Table 1 row data:** Healthcare (imaging, EHR/tabular, clinical text, signals, genomics) | GANs, VAEs, Transformers, diffusion models, federated generative models (with DP/PATE/secure aggregation for privacy) | Not reported — no big-data platform used; argues for edge AI and efficient (quantised/distilled) generative models | No primary data (p. 60). Cited benchmarks: MIMIC-III/IV, eICU, ISIC 2018–2020, BraTS, IXI, MSD, IU-Xray, UCR, ECG/EEG; scales up to 250k+ patients, 15M abstracts, 20 hospitals | First unified comparative evaluation of five generative families for healthcare synthetic data, mapping fidelity/privacy/compute trade-offs and regulation, with a cross-domain quantitative table.

## Synthesis hooks

- **Theme placement:** Data quality & governance (landscape-report branch B5: Privacy, governance and evaluation) — the pool's only source on synthetic-data generation, treating privacy (DP, federated, leakage) and evaluation (fidelity vs utility) as one governance problem.
- **Agrees with:** AIR-5 — reproducibility and attack-aware evaluation belong in appraisal, and fidelity metrics alone are not enough (pp. 6, 16, 53); AIR-2 — both are dataset-free secondary reviews that land on fragmented evaluation practice as the field's core weakness (pp. 6, 16, 39, 46); BDR-2 — federated learning's costs are communication/synchronisation overhead and non-IID degradation, needing fixes like SDS (pp. 41–42, 46, 55–56); JoBD-2 — federated generative models with DP let institutions share synthetic data without raw records, while gradient leakage remains a residual risk (pp. 40–42, 46, 57); TBD-5 — downstream/real-data benchmarks (TSTR) and synthetic augmentation for rare classes are the right validation route (p. 50); AIR-3 — cross-modal alignment is immature and multimodal fusion is the frontier (p. 31, p. 59).
- **Contradicts:** AIR-2 on evidence standard — counted PRISMA selection vs an explicitly uncounted, unscored survey (pp. 4, 6), so AIR-4's base is not auditable to the same standard; AIR-5 on depth — AIR-4 covers multivariate time-series generation with only two benchmark rows (ECG, EEG, p. 50) and performs no reproducibility audit; AIR-1 on scope — AIR-4's taxonomy is organised solely by generative training paradigm, with no agentic/tool-use dimension; TBD-2/TBD-4 on task — AIR-4 frames Transformers as text/image generators, not multivariate time-series classifiers, so its model-selection guidance does not transfer; and any pool work treating federated learning alone as sufficient privacy protection conflicts with AIR-4's gradient-leakage caveat (pp. 46, 57).
- **Extends/enables:** JoBD-2, BDR-2 (privacy machinery and federated cost analysis) — AIR-4 supplies the generative-model side and the leakage caveats; JoBD-1, JoBD-4, JoBD-3, AIR-3 (data-hungry healthcare/multimodal applications) — synthetic data is the acquisition path when real data is scarce or restricted; TBD-5 (rare-class streaming anomalies) — its synthetic-rare-anomaly recommendation is the same idea AIR-4 evaluates for clinical data; BDR-4/BDR-1 (risk/security) — synthetic-data detection is flagged as an open forensic problem (p. 35).
- **Unique contribution:** The pool's only unified comparison of GANs, VAEs, Transformers, diffusion and federated generative models for healthcare data, with a cross-domain quantitative table (Table 13), a use-case→model decision table (Table 12), and an explicit mapping of generative choices onto HIPAA/GDPR/EU AI Act/FDA/MHRA/WHO/OECD positions.
- **Quotable line:** "Importantly, while visual fidelity metrics such as FID and SSIM are widely reported, they do not always align with downstream diagnostic performance or clinical trustworthiness."
